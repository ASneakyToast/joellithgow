## About This Opportunity

Cognition's Special Projects Engineer role is described as "equal parts Engineering, Product, and GTM" — a cross-functional role requiring founder mentality. Special Projects Engineers own end-to-end implementations with strategic customers, translate customer pain into product improvements, and build internal tools that improve GTM and EPD efficiency. The role requires 2+ years experience, high agency, and comfort with ambiguity. Up to 25% travel.

This is for people who don't fit neatly into one function and thrive when given broad ownership.

## Why I'm Excited

1. **Cross-Functional by Nature** — My career spans QA, design, and engineering. I've never been just one thing, and this role doesn't ask me to be.
2. **Founder Mentality** — I co-founded and operate a gallery space in SF. I understand building from scratch with limited resources.
3. **End-to-End Ownership** — I've shipped entire projects solo: design system to deployment, concept to team adoption.
4. **Internal Tooling** — I've built automation tools, CMS workflows, and deployment pipelines. Building internal tools that improve team efficiency is something I do naturally.
5. **AI Domain Expertise** — Daily AI coding tool user at a company building AI coding tools. I understand the product space from the inside.

## Unique Angle

The "equal parts Engineering, Product, and GTM" framing is rare in job descriptions because most people aren't equal parts those things. I am:

- **Engineering:** TypeScript/Python daily, Docker/CI/CD, production deployments, creative coding
- **Product:** UX design background, user research, stakeholder management, data-driven decisions
- **GTM:** Ran the gallery's public-facing operations, created marketing materials, managed community engagement

This isn't a stretch — it's how I've worked for years. At Cognition, I'd bring that cross-functional comfort to navigating between customer needs, product direction, and engineering execution.

The founder angle is real, too. Running a gallery taught me resource constraints, decision-making under uncertainty, and the difference between planning and shipping. Those lessons apply directly to special projects work.
